What happens after blood leaves the big vessels?

Large arteries carry blood toward organs. They branch into smaller arteries and then arterioles. Blood passes through capillary networks near the tissues and continues into venules on its return journey. Together, the arterioles, capillaries and venules form the microcirculation.

From the highway to the neighborhood

Imagine looking at a country from space. The major highways are visible, but that view cannot tell you whether a particular household received its supplies. Large vessels are essential routes. To understand what happens near the tissue, we also have to look at the smaller local network.

Capillaries bring blood close to cells, where much of the exchange of oxygen, nutrients and waste products occurs. Red blood cells usually remain within the capillary; oxygen moves out of the blood and through the surrounding tissue. The analogy helps explain the route, but a visible or open large vessel alone does not establish what is happening in each capillary bed.

How oxygen crosses to tissue

Oxygen moves from blood toward tissue by diffusion along a difference in oxygen partial pressure. Blood pressure helps drive circulation, and pressure differences affect fluid movement across capillary walls. They do not simply push oxygen through the wall in the way a pump pushes water through a pipe.

Tissue oxygenation depends on several connected factors: the amount of blood arriving, its distribution through small vessels, the oxygen gradient, the condition of the surrounding tissue and the cells' ability to use oxygen. These factors can differ by tissue and circumstance.

What a pulse oximeter tells us

A pulse oximeter gives a useful estimate of arterial blood oxygen saturation from the measurement site. It does not directly measure oxygen use in every tissue. A normal reading therefore cannot, on its own, establish that all tissues are receiving and using oxygen adequately. It also is not evidence by itself that a hidden circulation problem exists. The reading belongs in a wider clinical picture.

Why the distinction matters

Microcirculation is a fundamental part of how tissues receive materials from blood. Its importance does not mean poor microcirculation causes every illness. Understanding the network gives us a more precise question to ask, while diagnosis and treatment still depend on the person's actual condition and the evidence.

Key takeaway: Large vessels bring blood toward an organ. Microcirculation helps determine what happens closer to the tissue itself.
